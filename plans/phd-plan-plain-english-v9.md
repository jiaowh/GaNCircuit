# PhD Research Plan — Plain English Version

**Working title:** What does a learned circuit model *use*, as opposed to merely *contain* — and does that gap, rather than accuracy, decide whether it can tell which AI-designed circuits survive layout and manufacturing variation?

**Candidate:** part-time, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. **Proposed supervisor:** Prof. Yang Chai (Applied Physics). **Co-supervisor:** to be named in electronic design automation or machine learning (§11).

Dated 2 September 2026. This is the plain-language version; the technical plan has the same content with the jargon left in.

---

## 1. The problem in one paragraph

Analog chip design is still done by hand. It is slow, and there are not many people who can do it. A lot of recent research points AI at the problem, and the headline numbers look excellent — until you read what is being measured. Across the established benchmark suites of basic analog blocks (the AnalogCoder tasks: amplifiers, mirrors, oscillators and the like), the best agent system now reports a 97.4% first-try pass rate with GPT-5 (AnalogAgent). A framework that starts from a schematic image and runs through netlist extraction, sizing, placement and routing reports 92.9% first try and 99.9% within five tries on fifteen circuits with GPT-5 (AnalogMaster) — and "pass" there means the netlist was right, the sizing met its targets at the typical corner, and the layout obeyed the process's geometric rules; the paper does not re-simulate the design with the layout's parasitics, so the performance being scored is still the drawing-board performance. The newest multimodal benchmark for analog design, AMSbench, scores a model's *understanding* of schematics and specifications; it too stops before silicon. The lower numbers that circulate — a little over 60% — come from a different problem entirely: choosing the topology of a small switched power converter to hit a target voltage ratio and efficiency (AutoCircuit-RL, which reports over 60% success at synthesising valid topologies with limited training data; EXPLORE, which adds search to the same problem). None of these numbers scores a design on how it behaves once the wiring's parasitics are included. The one recent end-to-end system that does report post-layout results (PANDA) shows the issue in a single design: gain and phase margin survive layout, but the unity-gain bandwidth falls from 10.84 MHz to 4.19 MHz against an 8 MHz target, and the paper says the frequency target is not fully maintained post-layout. The design passed on the drawing board with a 35% margin and missed once drawn. Nobody knows how often that happens.

The most recent sizing agent, AutoSizer (ICML 2026), reports 100% success on all three difficulty tiers of its own twenty-four-circuit SkyWater 130 nm benchmark under a 300-simulation budget — at the drawing-board stage. Its released code contains a post-layout check that the paper does not exercise (§2).

## 2. The gap nobody is covering

There are two research communities here, and they have started talking to each other.

**Community A** builds AI that invents circuit designs and sizes them. Historically they stopped at the drawing-board stage, and the newest ones still do: AaLLM (August 2026) invents op-amp and filter topologies, sizes them, reports that its sizing loop meets the target specification in 91.6% of cases, and outputs a netlist. No layout. The surveys of language models for design automation (TODAES 2025) and of circuit foundation models describe the same stopping point across the field.

**Community B** studies what happens when a design becomes a physical chip — the tiny unwanted resistances and capacitances the metal wiring adds, the fact that no two manufactured transistors are identical, and the way behaviour drifts with temperature and voltage. These effects routinely break a design that looked perfect on the drawing board.

Some recent systems take an AI-generated design through automated layout. PANDA extracts the parasitics and reports post-layout performance. AnalogMaster generates the layout and checks it against the process rules but, as far as its paper shows, never re-simulates with the extracted parasitics — it reaches the layout without scoring it. Layout-aware sizing tools have existed for years, and one that optimises post-layout performance directly was published in 2024 (Gao et al.). So the claim "nobody connects these two things" is false, and this plan does not make it.

**What is already published on manufacturing variation.** Variation-aware sizing frameworks exist and verify against industrial corner and Monte Carlo suites. GLOVA (DAC 2025) sizes circuits at 28 nm and verifies across 30 process–voltage–temperature corners, plus a hundred local-mismatch Monte Carlo samples on each of those corners, plus a thousand global-and-local samples on six voltage–temperature corners. It reports "success rates", but those are the fraction of *optimisation runs* that terminate in a design passing full verification: 100% for GLOVA, 87–100% for PVTSizing, 53–100% for RobustAnalog. Its headline figures of 80.5× and 76.0× are sample-efficiency and runtime multipliers, not pass rates; they are easy to misread as pass rates in the seventy-to-eighty percent range, and they are not. No layout is involved in GLOVA. EEsizer's robustness evidence is a ten-sample variation test on a single design. So the population statistic this plan measures is unpublished even at the schematic level, and unpublished with layout.

**Why it will not stay unpublished.** Two groups already hold the raw material. GLOVA's workflow first generates designs that pass at the typical condition and then simulates each across sampled mismatch under all corners, so the survival fraction is sitting in its logs. More directly, AutoSizer's public repository has a full-flow mode that sends the best pre-layout design of each iteration through ALIGN layout, Magic parasitic extraction and a second simulation, and records a pre-versus-post degradation for every metric. The paper runs its benchmark pre-layout only, and ALIGN currently covers a subset of its circuits (the simple OTAs and amplifiers). Producing the population number from that code is a flag flip and a few days of compute for a group that already owns the harness. That sets the clock in §10: preprint by month four, submission by month six.

**The gap that is still open is a measurement one.** The end-to-end systems report their best result — the one design that came out well. Nobody reports the *population* number: of all the designs an AI produces that pass on the drawing board, what fraction still meet specification after layout, and after manufacturing variation and temperature are included? And how does that fraction change as the specification margin tightens? That curve is the honest exchange rate between the field's headline scores and reality. The post-layout-aware sizing literature is where it would most plausibly hide. Gao et al. (2024) has been read and reports best-of-run performance per method, not a population statistic; Liu 2021 and Budak 2023 are still to be checked before submission.

**The physical-signoff gap, made explicit.** "After layout" is not one check. First, parasitic extraction turns the drawn wires into a circuit model with their real resistance, capacitance and coupling; this can move poles, slow a signal, inject noise and reduce stability. Second, process, voltage and temperature corners test systematic shifts. Third, Monte Carlo mismatch gives nominally identical transistors slightly different properties and asks for the fraction of manufactured chips that pass. These are separate labels, and the study will report them separately: a design may fail from its wiring even at the typical corner, from a corner even with benign wiring, or only from mismatch. Longer-term reliability effects such as aging, stress and time-dependent drift are a fourth layer. They are important, but are deliberately outside the first paper because the open 130 nm flow and the existing benchmarks do not provide a validated aging model; this is stated as a boundary, not silently treated as solved.

**The same three layers, where they are not a nuisance but the whole problem.** In a computing-in-memory macro — a memory array that performs the multiply-and-accumulate operations of a neural network by reading rows and summing currents down columns — the three signoff layers *are* the design problem. The wire resistance along word and bit lines produces voltage drops that limit how large an array can be read reliably; device-to-device variation appears as programming and read noise on every stored weight; temperature and drift move the stored conductances after programming; and the analog-to-digital converters that read the columns take up to 30% of the area and 50% of the power in one published resistive-memory design and as much as 58% of the energy and 81% of the area in another (Wolters et al., 2024). In such a circuit a specification is variation-limited by construction. That is why the model studied here is applied to a computing-in-memory tile in Year 3 (§7, Question 6; §10): the effects this plan measures are marginal in an op-amp and central there. The reasons it is not the *first* circuit family are practical and are given in §10.1.

**The missing loop is correction, not only diagnosis.** A normal AI circuit designer proposes a schematic and stops when its pre-layout simulation passes. The proposed pipeline sends that candidate through layout, extraction and variation checks, then feeds the *difference* back: for example, "the extracted wiring cut bandwidth by 35%" or "input offset fails because the matched pair is too sensitive to mismatch." The learned change model predicts which sizing or layout-aware change is likely to restore the lost margin. Only the most promising repairs pay for another expensive physical check. This makes the research question stronger than "does layout hurt?": can an AI learn to anticipate and repair the specific physical effect that caused the failure? The loop is defined concretely in §7 (Question 6) and is the Year-3 deliverable in §10; it is also the piece of this plan that matches the original project scope, an orchestration tool that iterates a design against an engineer's specification.

**Why it has not been published.** The obvious answer — compute cost, hours per design — is wrong for the nominal check. OSIRIS produces a rule-clean, LVS-verified layout with extraction and simulation in 25 to 48 seconds per layout on average, depending on the circuit, on a 32-core workstation; in the same paper ALIGN produced a complete single-pass layout for each of the five circuits in roughly a minute, including the low-pass filter on which MAGICAL failed. The published post-layout sizing loop of Gao et al. (2024), with MAGICAL as the layout generator on a 40 nm amplifier, reports per iteration 70 ± 50 seconds for layout generation and 80 ± 15 seconds for post-layout simulation, against 25 ± 5 seconds for the pre-layout simulation; on its regulator the figures are 20 ± 15 and 65 ± 15 seconds. An older data point agrees: AutoCkt reported 2.4 seconds for a schematic simulation and about 91 seconds with layout parasitics through BAG. Two cautions on the OSIRIS figure: it is a mean over placement-perturbation iterations around an already-placed layout, and the simulated-annealing placement for a fresh sizing is not timed separately, so the per-design cost for new sizings must be measured, not assumed — ALIGN's minute is the better anchor for a fresh layout. Even at several minutes per design, a few hundred fixed-topology designs across corners is days of workstation time. The check becomes hours per design only with full statistical (Monte Carlo) simulation. The real barrier is human time: every automated layout tool needs per-circuit setup — OSIRIS takes a netlist template, a testbench and a list of matched transistor pairs — and the tools fail on unfamiliar circuits. That barrier shapes the study design in §10: the first population is AI-chosen *sizings* of known circuit families, not AI-invented topologies.

## 3. The idea

Teach a model to *predict the consequence of a design change* without doing the expensive check.

Existing AI models answer "how good is this circuit?" I want a model that answers "if I make this specific change, what happens?" The model takes a design and a proposed change and outputs the change in behaviour. This is the primary formulation throughout. The plain "design in, response out" model is one of the competitors in Question 4, not the main object.

Two reasons for the change-based framing, stated at their true strength. It is what a human expert does: they do not simulate every idea; they know from experience what most changes will do, and simulate the ones they are unsure about. And it puts the quantity the thesis cares about — the model's response to a change — directly on the training target. What the framing does *not* do is make the central test in §4 possible: a plain forward model gives the predicted change as the difference of two predictions, so the intervention test applies to every model in Question 4 equally. The change-based model is a hypothesis about what trains better, tested in Question 4, not a precondition.

**Where the change-based framing comes from, and what it buys.** It is not new outside circuits. Pairwise difference regression (Tynes et al., 2021, chemistry) trains a model on the differences between all pairs of training points rather than on the points themselves, which turns N designs into roughly N² training examples and gives an uncertainty from the spread of pair predictions; a 2024 study found the pairwise formulation markedly better at extrapolating beyond the range of the training targets. Extrapolation is exactly the regime where the shortcut story of §4 lives, so the change-based model has a prior reason to differ from the plain model, not just an analogy to human practice. Inside circuits I found no prior change-prediction model; that is a gap, not a validation.

**The simulator supplies consequence labels for free.** ngspice can compute the sensitivity of any output — how much it moves per unit change of every device and model parameter — for the DC operating point or the small-signal frequency response, by perturbing each parameter in turn. Sobolev training (Czarnecki et al., 2017) trains a network on such derivative targets alongside the usual value targets and reports higher accuracy and stronger generalisation. So the change-based model can be trained on values plus the simulator's own sensitivities along *some* directions and tested on directions it never saw. That is the sharpest operational meaning of "uses" versus "contains" on offer: a model that merely contains gain will get the held-out directions wrong. The idea has a five-year head start one level down: the community that replaces transistor equations with neural networks (BSIM-NN, the gate-all-around ANN model, NeuroSPICE) trains on derivatives because a device model with correct current but wrong small-signal slopes does not converge in a circuit simulator. That lineage is cited, and which of those works use an explicit derivative loss is checked before submission. One caveat: whether ngspice's sensitivity command works through the SKY130 device wrappers is unverified; finite differences by re-simulation are the fallback and cost seconds per design.

**What the model is.** Primary skeleton: one model over all circuit families, taking the netlist as a graph of devices and wires plus the sizing, in the style of CktGNN (ICLR 2023), FALCON's edge-centric network, or INSIGHT's tokenised transformer (DAC 2025), trained across families and tested on families it never saw. Per-family models — a small network per topology — are run as a control, because on a fixed family with four to twenty-six sizing variables a small network fits nominal behaviour almost exactly and the read-versus-use question becomes trivial; the per-family control bounds how much of any gap is representation rather than capacity. Other learned circuit models this plan's questions apply to, and which are treated as instances of arm (A) in Question 4 rather than as novelties: INSIGHT, Learn-by-Compare (DAC 2024, a contrastive-regression encoder for sizing solutions), CktGNN, and DICE (a contrastive circuit encoder).

**What the model is not: the device model is fixed.** Everything above sits on top of the process's transistor models, which are treated as ground truth throughout. That is a deliberate boundary, because the device model is itself a live research target: neural-network compact models now replace the transistor equations (BSIM-NN; a dual-network model that reaches 0.8% drain-current error, runs inside a commercial simulator faster than the standard Verilog-A model, and evaluates 18.8 million DC points per second on a graphics card for variability analysis — Novkin and Amrouch, 2025), and the extraction of standard-model parameters is itself done by neural networks (iPREFER; a transformer that outputs only the binnable parameters of the industry gate-all-around model to under 5% error — Kang et al., 2026). Learned device models enter this plan in exactly two places: as a cheap fidelity level in the Year-3 search loop (Question 6), never as the ground truth for any claim; and as the Year-2 bridge to the supervisor's devices (§10.3), where the group's measured device is given a simulator-ready statistical model so that the Year-3 computing-in-memory tile can be simulated with it. The binning result also explains why §9 snaps sizings to the process's model bins and logs the snap: binning is a modelling artefact with its own error, not a formality.

**The nearest existing thing, and why this is not it.** FALCON (NeurIPS 2025) builds a learned forward model from topology and parameters to performance on a dataset of over one million Cadence-simulated circuits, then works backwards through it by gradients to choose parameters, with layout entering the objective as differentiable constraints and costs. That is the same shape of idea. My change-based model is, in effect, a learned version of the forward model's derivative — the very object FALCON differentiates through. Any proposal in this area has to say how it differs, and this one differs in three ways, in order of how much they matter:

1. **It is never asked what it knows.** FALCON is evaluated on whether its answers are right, not on what it internally represents or which properties it actually uses. Every question in §6 is about the second thing. This is the real separation and it does not depend on the other two.
2. **Layout enters as extraction, not as a formula.** FALCON's layout awareness is a differentiable layout cost derived from analytical equations for parasitic and frequency-dependent effects, constrained by design rules. Mine comes from a generated layout with extracted parasitics, which is a different and checkable kind of ground truth.
3. **The circuits are different.** FALCON's are mm-wave designs simulated through Cadence Spectre, twenty expert-designed topologies in five families (a secondary summary gives 45 nm at 30 GHz; confirm from the paper before submission); mine are op-amp families in an open 130 nm process, and later a computing-in-memory tile. Its topologies are human-designed and fixed.

If a reviewer says "this has been done", point at 1. Points 2 and 3 are supporting, not load-bearing. FALCON is also a competitor in Question 6, and its public dataset is used directly in Question 5 (§7).

## 4. The catch, and why it makes this a PhD rather than a software project

There is a recent finding in AI research about what predictive models fail to learn: Tan et al., "What Can Latent World Models Know?", arXiv 2607.27017 (v1 29 July 2026; an arXiv preprint at the time of writing). Getting it right matters, because it is easy to build on the wrong half of it, and easy to overstate what an experiment in circuits would test against it. Both traps are marked below.

**What the finding says.** Train a model to predict the future of a physical scene where each object has hidden properties (mass, drag, stiffness) that are re-drawn every episode and never visible. Some properties enter the model and some never do. The ones that fail share two features: they reveal themselves slowly, and reading them out of what the model can observe requires *dividing* one signal by another rather than combining signals linearly. The second feature describes the relationship between the property and the model's sensors, not the property alone. The mechanism the authors propose is simple: when the model is trained to predict a single best-guess future, anything a short history does not pin down is best answered with the population average, so nothing pushes the model to form a belief about this particular object. Piling on more data does not help; making the property a prediction target does — a supervised head on the same trunk lifts the blind property from about 0.12 to 0.45 with prediction quality unchanged. In a coordinate system where the property's trace becomes linear (a log-speed channel), the property became partly *readable* from the model — but the model still did not *use* it in its own forecasts.

**Two mechanisms, not one, and what the models are.** The paper's own summary is that inputs limit what can be known while prediction targets decide what is retained: a sensor stream that is fed in but never forecast contributes nothing (touch as input left stiffness at chance; touch as a prediction target recovered it), and the blind region above is a separate, third finding. Both matter below, because they apply to different circuit properties. The models are JEPAs — joint-embedding predictive architectures that forecast the model's own internal representation rather than raw observations, built on the LeJEPA/LeWM recipe with a "SIGReg" anti-collapse regulariser, about five million parameters each, the whole study on one RTX 4060. The paper reports the same blind region for a pixel-reconstruction variant on the same trunk, so the finding is about deterministic point-prediction objectives generally, not about JEPAs in particular.

**Two things the paper already tested.** First, the authors tried one distributional objective: a model that predicts a mean and a variance (a heteroscedastic Gaussian likelihood). It moved nothing on drag (0.10 versus 0.12), because its best answer for the *mean* is still the population mean. Second, they state their open question precisely: objectives whose optimum is *not* a conditional mean — episode-level latent variables, belief states. "Predict a distribution" is not, by itself, that open question. This matters for claim 2(ii) below.

**Why the direct version does not transfer.** My model takes a circuit and its sizing as input. Gain is a ratio, stability margin is a ratio, but neither is hidden: they are computed from inputs the model has been handed in full, and there is no "episode" with a re-drawn secret. The mechanism above has nothing to grip. Testing it would return a clean "works fine" that teaches no one anything.

**Why the read-versus-use gap does not transfer as-is either.** In the source, "read but not used" is a symptom of the same mechanism: a linear trace of the hidden property leaks into the model, but predicting the future never needed it. Remove the hidden property and you remove the reason for the gap. It might appear in circuits anyway, but for a different reason, and the plan has to name it.

**What can produce a read-versus-use gap in circuits: shortcut learning.** Within the training circuits, some cheap feature — the width of one transistor, say — happens to move together with gain. The model learns to predict from the shortcut. A probe still "finds" gain inside the model, because gain moves together with the output. The gap shows up the moment a sizing change moves gain by a route the shortcut does not track. So in circuits, "does the model use gain?" means: **does the model's predicted response to a sizing change that moves gain match the simulator's?** That is the test, and it is cheap, because the simulator's sensitivity to every sizing parameter costs seconds at the drawing-board stage. Stated honestly, this gap is a per-property breakdown of how the model fails outside its training distribution. That breakdown, and whether it predicts usefulness, is the contribution. I will not dress it up as something else. This mechanism does not depend on Tan et al.; it is the standard shortcut-learning account (Geirhos et al., 2020). Tan et al. supply the framing and the measurement protocol. If the preprint fails to replicate or is overturned, claim 2(i) stands on its own.

**Where variation actually sits in Tan et al.'s map — and it is not the blind region.** In the open 130 nm process, each transistor's mismatch enters the model as a random draw whose size scales as one over the square root of its area (width × length × multiplicity). The *spread* of a design's offset is therefore a fixed function of the sizing: the inputs carry it completely. What is hidden is the *individual chip's draw*. So the paper's two mechanisms come apart cleanly in circuits:

- The spread is input-visible. It can fail to be learned only through *target pressure*: a model trained on individual simulated chips to predict a single best-guess response learns the average response and has no reason to keep the spread, exactly as touch fed in but never forecast contributed nothing. The cures are objectives whose optimum depends on the spread. The cheapest is a model that predicts a best guess *and* a spread from the same per-chip samples (a heteroscedastic Gaussian likelihood): its best answer for the spread is the true spread, so nothing collapses. Tan et al.'s null result for that objective was about a hidden parameter that shifts the mean, and does not transfer. This arm is C-het in Question 4. It is a control, not a finding — but it is the budget-defining control, because it needs a handful of chips per design pooled across many designs rather than hundreds of chips per design (§13).
- The individual draw is hidden. Whether a model with a per-chip latent variable, trained on individual chips with the spread never a target, recovers the draw in its latent and the spread in its trunk is the source paper's open question — objectives whose optimum is not a conditional mean. That is C2. Unlike the source paper, the ground truth for the hidden variable is available here (§8), so the answer can be certified rather than inferred.

**What an honest test of the open question looks like.** Three arms, all in Question 4. C-het (predict best guess and spread from per-chip samples) and C1 (the spread computed from hundreds of chips as an explicit label) are the paper's supervised-head arm in two costs; both are expected to work and are controls, not findings. C2 is a model with a per-chip latent variable, trained only on individual simulated chips, where the chip's draw is never given as an input and the spread is never a target. If C2 recovers spread-defined properties where the single-best-guess model does not, and if its latent tracks the true draw, that answers the open question in a domain where the ground truth is exact. If it does not, that is reported too.

**JEPA in circuits, honestly.** The original project brief asked for a model that understands circuits "JEPA-style or by whatever means possible". The honest assessment: a JEPA's motivation — refuse to predict unpredictable pixel detail, forecast in representation space instead — has little grip on a dozen exact scalars that cost a second each to label, so its self-supervised advantage is weak here. What a latent-predictive arm *can* test is one of Tan et al.'s central claims in circuit form: pretrain a latent world model on cheap drawing-board transitions (design, sizing change → next design's representation) and ask whether post-layout-only properties — the pole shifts that only extraction reveals — appear in the latent without ever being forecast. The paper predicts they will not. That is a falsifiable, domain-specific transfer of "fusion without forecasting contributes nothing", and a natural use of the same probes. It is arm D in Question 4, explicitly non-load-bearing; if it is cut for time, nothing else in the plan moves.

**Why circuits are still the ideal place to work on this.** Simulation gives exact ground truth for free, including the true sensitivity to every design change, the true manufacturing distribution, and — unlike the source paper's robot data — the true value of the hidden per-chip variable itself. Analog engineers already work in logarithmic units, so the coordinate question can be settled cheaply. And the models involved are small: the source's entire study, about five million parameters per model, ran on one consumer graphics card.

**Before anything else is built,** the premise gets tested. Months one to three: build the table of candidate circuit properties against my observation setup, and classify each as visible or hidden in the inputs, ratio-type or linear, and whether the training target's best answer for it is a population mean. Expected result: nominal properties (gain, margin, bandwidth) are visible and no mechanism applies; spread-defined properties (yield, offset spread) are *visible in the inputs but unrewarded by a single-best-guess target*, so the target-pressure mechanism applies; the individual chip's draw is the only genuinely hidden quantity, and the per-episode latent question applies to it alone. That expected split is what the rest of the project is built on, and it is published as a table either way.

So the project has a real question with an unknown answer: **for which circuit properties does a model's predicted response to a design change come apart from what a probe can read out of it and from its ordinary accuracy — and does that gap, not accuracy, decide whether the model is any use for guiding a design search?**

## 5. What I will actually claim

Two claims, both specific to circuits.

**First (measurement):** of the AI-sized analog designs that meet specification on the drawing board, the fraction that still meets it after extracted layout parasitics and across process, voltage and temperature corners — with mismatch reported as its own Monte Carlo survival measure — differs between generators, families and layout policies. The results are reported as curves against specification margin, per generator and per circuit family, under two fixed and named layout policies, with failures separated into wiring/parasitics, systematic corners and local mismatch. Which designs survive is predictable from properties visible at the drawing-board stage, with the predictor holding on families and generators it was not fitted on. That the fraction falls as the margin tightens is expected of any noisy transformation and is not the claim; the shape of the curves, the decomposition of failure causes, the gap between the two layout policies, and the out-of-sample predictor are.

**Second (learnability):** in a learned circuit model, the per-property intervention error — the mismatch between the model's predicted response to a sizing change that moves property X and the simulator's — comes apart from aggregate accuracy and from probe readability, in three predictable ways.

(i) For properties fully determined by the inputs, the gap is shortcut learning, and which properties are affected is set by which circuits and sizings the training set covers, not by how many there are.

(ii) For properties defined on the manufacturing distribution, a model trained on individual simulated chips with a single-best-guess objective is blind to them regardless of coverage or quantity — probes and the intervention test both fail — even though the inputs determine them. Any objective whose optimum depends on the spread restores them: an explicit spread label, or a best-guess-plus-spread likelihood on the same per-chip samples. Those are the known cure, run as controls. Whether a per-chip latent-variable objective that never sees the spread as a target also restores them, and whether its latent recovers the true per-chip draw, is the open question from the source paper, and it is answered either way.

(iii) When the specification is limited by layout or by variation, the per-property intervention error, not aggregate accuracy, predicts how many expensive checks a model-guided search needs. This is tested twice: on op-amp specifications engineered to be layout- or variation-limited, and on a computing-in-memory tile, where every specification is variation-limited by construction.

Part (i) is the shortcut mechanism, new to this setting. Part (ii) is the source paper's target-pressure mechanism transplanted, with two controls and one test; only the test is a finding. Part (iii) is the link to practice; its nearest neighbour is a 2026 sizing paper that states no prior work quantifies how prediction accuracy relates to convergence — that work relates *aggregate* accuracy to convergence, and the claim here is that the per-property breakdown does better. None of the three is a restatement of the source paper's conclusion.

## 6. Questions the project answers

0. **(Premise check, months 1–3.)** For each candidate circuit property under my observation setup: is it determined by the inputs, does reading it require division, does a single-best-guess target reward it, and is it hidden? Expected: nominal properties visible and rewarded; spread-defined properties visible but unrewarded; only the per-chip draw hidden.
1. Does a property get learned only if the model is explicitly asked to predict it, or is showing it as an input enough? Run as an explicit grid: inputs (netlist and sizing; plus drawing-board response; plus post-layout response) crossed with targets (drawing-board; post-layout; individual post-manufacturing chips under a best-guess objective; the same chips under a best-guess-plus-spread objective; the spread as an explicit label).
2. For which properties can a probe read a quantity out of the model while the model's predicted response to changes in that quantity is wrong? Does training on the simulator's sensitivities along some directions fix the response on held-out directions? Does changing to logarithmic coordinates — on inputs as well as outputs — close that gap, open it, or move it?
3. Do different kinds of simulation combine? Does asking about DC behaviour, frequency response and manufacturing variation together teach the model more than any one alone?
4. Is the change-based training style the right choice, and what does the objective change? Seven styles on identical simulation budgets, tuned equally hard: (A) a plain regression model, design in, response out, instantiated as a published learned circuit model (INSIGHT- or CktGNN-style), not a bespoke network; (B) the change-based model of §3; (B-S) the change-based model trained additionally on the simulator's sensitivities along a fixed subset of directions; (C-het) the change-based model predicting best guess and spread from individual chips; (C1) the change-based model with the spread from hundreds of chips as an explicit target; (C2) the change-based model with a per-chip latent variable, trained on individual chips, spread never a target; (D) an action-conditioned latent-predictive model in the source paper's recipe, with the sizing change as the action. Load-bearing comparisons: B versus A (framing), C-het and C1 versus the best-guess grid cell (target pressure), C2 (open question), B-S versus B (whether derivative supervision closes the read-versus-use gap). D is exploratory.
5. Does *which* training circuits you choose matter more than how many? Crossed with an explicit quantity axis: four training-set sizes spanning a factor of ten, for each selection strategy. Run twice: on my own simulations (with intervention tests) and on FALCON's public million-circuit set (probes only, no new simulations).
6. Does the per-property intervention error predict usefulness? In the repair loop of §2, with the change model ranking candidate repairs, do models with low error on the layout- and variation-related properties save expensive checks on specifications that those properties limit, and do models with high error on them fail to? Answered on op-amp families first and on a computing-in-memory tile second.

## 7. How each question gets answered

Same model skeleton throughout (§3), changing one thing at a time.

**Question 0:** Build the table of candidate properties against the observation setup. For each, establish whether it is determined by the inputs, whether reading it requires division, whether the target's best answer for it is a population mean, and whether it is hidden. Report the table. It is a publishable object in its own right and nobody in circuits has one.

**Question 1:** Train one model per cell of the input-by-target grid. Compare what each version knows using the instruments below.

**Question 2:** Six instruments, fixed in advance, not chosen after seeing results:

- a simple linear readout attached to the model's internals;
- a more capable nonlinear readout, to separate "the model does not know this" from "my simple readout was too weak";
- a learnability control: a separate supervised head on the same skeleton, trained on the same data to predict the property directly (§8);
- the intervention test: for each property, sizing changes are chosen — using the simulator's own sensitivities — that move that property while leaving the other monitored properties roughly still; the model's predicted change is compared to the simulated change. A second set of changes moves the shortcut candidates without moving the property. A model that "uses" the property tracks the first set and ignores the second;
- the held-out-direction test: for arm B-S, the directions used in training are recorded and the intervention test is scored separately on directions the model was trained on and directions it was not. A gap between the two is the cleanest evidence of "contains but does not use";
- a control: the same readouts run against deliberately meaningless targets, to establish what score a readout gets purely from its own flexibility. Without that control, none of the other numbers mean anything.

Two details of the intervention test. First, "move X while holding the rest still" is a direction in the null space of the other properties' sensitivities, and that construction is exact only for small changes. Shortcut failures show up on large changes, outside the training distribution, where the linearisation breaks. For large changes the path is simulated, the held properties are checked afterwards, and any co-movement is reported and regressed out rather than assumed away. Second, shortcut candidates are not left to chance. They are identified two ways: by correlation analysis within each training distribution, and by construction — dedicated training sets in which a cheap feature is deliberately made to move with a property, so that a gap can be attributed to the shortcut rather than to accident. The coverage test in §11 uses those constructed sets.

Repeat the whole battery in ordinary units and in logarithmic units, on inputs (widths, lengths, currents) as well as outputs (decibels, decades).

**Question 3:** Train with one type of simulation as the target, then combinations. Measure what each adds.

**Question 4:** Run all seven styles side by side on identical simulation budgets. For C-het, C1 and C2, the readouts and the intervention test are run on the distribution-defined properties first, because that is where the best-guess cells are expected to be blind. C-het and C1 passing is expected and establishes that the data supported the property; C2 is the result, scored twice: whether the spread is recoverable from its trunk, and whether its per-chip latent tracks the true draw (§8). For D, the readouts are run on post-layout-only properties after pretraining on drawing-board transitions alone; the source paper predicts absence.

**Question 5:** Compare purely random training circuits, circuits proposed by a language model that knows design conventions, and circuits deliberately chosen to sit near failure points, at each of four data-set sizes. The claim "coverage, not quantity" requires the quantity axis to be flat and the coverage axis not to be. The second run subsamples FALCON's public set — a million Cadence-simulated mm-wave circuits across twenty topologies in a proprietary 45 nm process — by the same three strategies; no simulator is available for that process, so only the probes run there, not the intervention test. Agreement between an open 130 nm op-amp set and a proprietary 45 nm mm-wave set would make the claim hard to dismiss as a process artefact.

**Question 6, on op-amps:** Use the model inside the repair loop of §2 on specifications that are post-layout- or variation-limited, so the properties in question are load-bearing; a search on drawing-board-limited specifications cannot show the gap. The loop is defined now. Action space: continuous sizing changes, plus the discrete layout-aware moves that OSIRIS exposes (transistor finger count; component shift within its placement halo). Proposer: a language model given the diagnosed failure ("extracted wiring cut bandwidth by 35%") and the netlist. Ranker: the change model, which predicts the consequence of each proposed repair. Only the top-ranked repairs pay for layout, extraction, corners or Monte Carlo. Count expensive checks needed to reach a working design. Three-way comparison on the same harness: the language model alone (AutoSizer-style meta-optimisation), the language model with the change-model ranker, and plain Bayesian optimisation on the expensive simulation. Further named competitors, in increasing order of difficulty: the multi-fidelity method of Zhang et al. (DAC 2019); the graph-network-plus-Bayesian method of Liu et al. (DATE 2021), which removes layout from the loop by predicting parasitics and is the same core idea as mine, five years old; the coupled pre/post-layout optimiser of Gao et al. (2024); INSIGHT's neural-simulator-driven reinforcement learning (DAC 2025); and FALCON's gradient-based inversion, the learned-forward-model competitor. A learned device model of the kind in §3 may be added as a cheap fidelity level for the multi-fidelity competitor; it is never the ground truth. I expect the competitors to be competitive and possibly to win, and I say so before running the experiment rather than after. That is reported either way. Question 6 is framed as a demonstration; the headline is whether the intervention error *predicted* which searches went badly.

**Question 6, on a computing-in-memory tile:** The same loop, same instruments, on one fixed tile family: a resistive-memory crossbar with its word-line and bit-line drivers, sense amplifiers and column analog-to-digital converters. Design variables: array size, converter resolution, the stored conductance range and number of levels, wire width and multiplicity, and the sizing of the sense path. Properties: the inference accuracy of a fixed small network mapped onto the tile, energy per multiply-accumulate, and read latency. The cheap check is the drawing-board tile model with ideal wires and nominal cells. The expensive check is a statistical array simulation with extracted wire resistance, per-cell programming and read noise drawn from the device model's variation parameters, and temperature; the same three-way failure decomposition — wiring, corners, per-cell mismatch — applies without change. The device model is the SkyWater 130 nm resistive-memory primitive if the Year-2 bridge (§10.3) does not deliver one for the group's own device, and the group's model if it does. Here every specification is variation-limited by construction, so the test of claim 2(iii) needs no engineered specifications. The population claim (claim 1) is *not* made for this family, for the reasons in §10.1: there is no external AI generator of such tiles to measure. Nearest competitors: the statistical simulators the field already uses (NeuroSim, CrossSim, AIHWKit) with their built-in noise models, and variation-aware training of the mapped network, which fixes the network rather than the tile.

## 8. The rule that keeps this honest

Before I can say "the model failed to learn X," I have to prove X was learnable in the first place. Otherwise a failure might just mean my training circuits never contained the information.

The source paper does this in two separate steps, and they mean different things here. The paper's *certificate* is a probe on the raw observations, before any model exists: is the property recoverable from what the sensors show at all? Its *supervised head* is a second control on the trained model's own trunk: does the trunk carry the property the moment the objective asks for it? For nominal and spread-defined properties the certificate is trivially satisfied — the netlist and sizing determine them through the simulator — so the meaningful control is the supervised head: a separate head on the same skeleton, trained on the same data to predict X directly. If that head reaches X, the information was there and this skeleton could extract it from this much data. Only then does a failure to learn it mean something.

For the per-chip draw the certificate is not trivial — it is exact, which is better than the source paper had. In the open 130 nm models the mismatch of each device is a named random variable added to its threshold voltage, oxide thickness and two other parameters, scaled by the device's area; the simulator's seed can be fixed and the draws logged, or the random terms replaced by explicit per-device offsets set by me. So for arm C2 the hidden variable's true value is known for every training chip: its latent can be probed against the actual draw, and matched pairs can be built exactly as the source paper builds them — same design, same stimulus, one device's draw changed. The certificate step is uninformative for the visible properties and decisive for the hidden one, and the thesis will say which is which.

The same discipline applies to the readouts themselves: every probe is also run against deliberately meaningless targets, so a probe score is only reported net of what the probe achieves on nonsense.

## 9. Measuring success properly

Replacing "did the simulator crash" with things that matter:

- Does the design meet its specification after the physical layout effects are included?
- **Of the designs that looked fine on the drawing board, what fraction still meets specification after layout and across corners — as a curve against specification margin?** This is the headline of the first paper. One number would be an anecdote: the fraction depends on the generator, the layout tool, the layout policy, the process and the margin, so it is reported as a curve, per generator and per circuit family, under each stated layout policy.
- **Two layout policies, fixed in advance and named.** OSIRIS shows that layouts of the *same* netlist differ by orders of magnitude in how much parasitic damage they do — on its Miller amplifier the single-pass MAGICAL layout scores 0.27 V of parasitic degradation against 0.0007 V for the best explored variant, with ALIGN's single-pass layout at 0.14 V. A survival fraction is therefore a property of the design *and* the layout policy. The first paper uses two single-pass policies, one layout per design under each: the OSIRIS baseline (sequence-pair placement solved with an integer program, simulated annealing on wire length, then shortest-path global routing and A* detailed routing) and ALIGN, which is the layout tool inside AutoSizer's full flow and produced valid layouts for all five OSIRIS circuits in about a minute each. The gap between the two curves is reported as the "layout skill" component of survival. Best-of-N over OSIRIS perturbations is a separate, reported condition, not the default. Transistor finger counts are a layout degree of freedom that OSIRIS shows changes parasitics; the rule is stated once — the minimum valid finger assignment that satisfies the matched-pair constraints — and applied to every design.
- **Sizings are snapped to the process's model bins before anything is simulated.** The open 130 nm models are binned by width and length, and community reports say Monte Carlo runs fail for off-bin geometries. An off-bin sizing is recorded as "sizer proposed an off-bin device" and snapped to the nearest bin; the snap is logged so its effect on survival can be reported.
- Does it still work across full manufacturing variation (Monte Carlo)?
- Is the drawing-board predictor of survival validated out of sample — fitted on some families and generators, tested on others?
- How does it compare to a published human design?
- How many expensive checks does it take to get there?
- For the computing-in-memory tile: does the intervention error on the variation properties predict the number of expensive array simulations the repair loop needs, and does the tile the loop arrives at hold its inference accuracy under the full statistical simulation?

## 10. Timeline

### 10.1 The choice of process and circuit families, and what the public data can and cannot do

The question "why SkyWater 130 nm op-amps, when larger public datasets exist and the supervisor's field is computing in memory" has a precise answer. The first paper is a statement about *new* designs produced by *existing* AI systems, checked through layout, extraction, corners and mismatch. That needs five things: device models that can be re-simulated, including Monte Carlo mismatch; an automatic layout tool for the circuit families; parasitic extraction; the process the measured generators natively run on — re-implementing AnalogSAGE, AutoSizer or OSIRIS on another process would measure my port, not their output; and, optionally, a shuttle. A dataset, however large, supplies none of the first three unless its process is open. A computing-in-memory tile fails the test on three of the five: there is no published AI generator of such tiles whose output could be measured, so there is no population; there is no automatic layout tool for them in the open flow, and the converter in the read path is the hardest analog block in the tile; and the emerging devices the supervisor's group makes have no simulator-ready statistical model in any public kit. That is why the tile is the Year-3 family for the learnability and search claims and not the Year-1 family for the measurement claim, and why Year 2 contains the bridge task in §10.3 that supplies the missing device model.

Open processes, as of September 2026:

- **SkyWater 130 nm (primary).** Mismatch models present (per-device random terms scaled by area, switched on with one parameter); layout through OSIRIS, ALIGN and glayout; extraction through Magic; the benchmarks whose outputs I am measuring live here (AnalogSAGE, AMS-SizingBench, OSIRIS, AnalogCoder-style tasks, and the LDO and amplifier categories of AnalogGym, which are the only AnalogGym categories on ngspice and an open process). It also ships a resistive-memory primitive with a Verilog-A model; whether that model carries statistical variation parameters is to be verified, and if it does not, a published resistive-memory statistical model is substituted for the Year-3 tile. Shuttles run again via ChipFoundry after Efabless closed in March 2025.
- **GlobalFoundries 180 nm.** Its statistical models are the best documented of the three: mismatch and global variation are separate switches with a documented skew parameter, and the global model is published with a 500-run Monte Carlo comparison against measured data. glayout generates its op-amp for either SkyWater 130 or GF 180 from one function. No AI-sizing benchmark suite lives natively here, so it cannot be a primary; and a process-transfer study is not part of this plan (§14).
- **IHP SG13G2 130 nm BiCMOS (later, not now).** Statistical model files with mismatch scaled by area exist for ngspice and Xyce, and a Monte Carlo flow is demonstrated by the JKU chip template; it adds silicon-germanium bipolar transistors. But the open analog flow is xschem/ngspice/KLayout with a Magic flow only planned, the PDK is early-access and not scheduled for production, the glayout port had basic primitives passing design rules but transistors not working as of late 2024, and the transistor models are PSP through OpenVAF, slower than the BSIM4 models of the other two for Monte Carlo budgets. A good third process once its layout path matures.
- **SkyWater 90 nm FDSOI.** Announced 2022, still an experimental preview with documentation marked work in progress. Not usable.
- **Predictive kits (ASAP7, FreePDK).** Public but with no fab, no mismatch models of the required kind and no analog layout automation. Not used.

Public datasets, and which question each can serve:

- **FALCON**: about a million Cadence-simulated mm-wave circuits across twenty topologies in a proprietary process. Netlists and sixteen performance metrics are public; the process is not. Serves Q1/Q2 probes on a large cross-topology model and Q5's coverage-versus-quantity axis. Cannot serve intervention tests, layout or Monte Carlo.
- **AICircuit** (same group): seven basic circuits and two transceiver systems, Cadence-simulated over swept parameters. Same limits as FALCON.
- **AnalogGenie (3,350 topologies), CktGNN's OCB, AMSNet, Masala-CHAI**: topology and netlist sets, the last two extracted from schematic images; pre-layout, mostly not re-simulatable in an open process. Topology sources only; not used in this plan's three papers (§14).
- **OSIRIS**: 87,100 SkyWater 130 nm layouts with paired pre- and post-layout simulations, parasitic degradation and placement metadata, at fixed sizing. The only public post-layout-labelled set.
- **AMS-SizingBench (24 circuits) and AnalogGym's open subset**: sizing environments, not datasets; populations come from running them.
- **OpenFASoC/glayout op-amp population**: a public generator that produces and extraction-simulates 1,700-plus variations of a two-stage op-amp on SkyWater 130 nm, with a tapeouts repository reporting measured results from manufactured chips for OpenFASoC-generated op-amps, regulators and temperature sensors. A public, silicon-measured *population* of one parametric family. It partly answers the "one chip is one sample" limit below: many designs on one process sample. How many op-amp variants were actually measured is to be read from the tapeout record before this is counted on.

### 10.3 The compact-model bridge (Year 2)

The supervisor's group publishes devices — two-dimensional ferroelectric-semiconductor transistors that implement synaptic learning rules in a single device, and sensors that compute on the incoming signal — with measured curves and behavioural fits, not with models a circuit simulator can run. That is the gap between the group's work and this plan, and closing it is a deliverable in its own right: **a simulator-ready, statistical compact model of one device the group has measured.**

Concretely: take the group's measured device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle data for one device; train a neural-network compact model of the current and charge as a function of terminal voltages and temperature, in the dual-network style of Novkin and Amrouch (a small network that maps device geometry and variation parameters to the weights of a current network), with the derivative constraints the compact-modelling community uses so that the model converges in a simulator; export it as Verilog-A or OSDI so that ngspice and commercial simulators can run it in Monte Carlo; validate it against held-out measured devices; and publish it with the data. This is a paper in the device-modelling venues on its own, it is what makes the Year-3 tile simulable with the group's own device, and it is the part of the thesis a device-physics committee reads as theirs.

Gate: the task exists only if the group holds, or will measure, variability data on a device and agrees to a student modelling and publishing it. If not, it is cut at the start of Year 2, the Year-3 tile runs on the SkyWater resistive-memory primitive or a published statistical model, and nothing in Years 1–2 moves.

### 10.4 On the chip

Optional. One chip from one shuttle run is a single sample from the manufacturing distribution — it can confirm that the nominal design works, but it cannot test a claim about *variation*. An agent-generated I/O ring has been fabricated and validated in a 28 nm tape-out (AMS-IO-Agent, AAAI 2026), described by its authors as the first agent-completed subtask with outputs used directly in silicon. That is pad-ring infrastructure rather than a signal-path circuit, so "first silicon for an AI-sized amplifier" may still be claimable — but it would be a demonstration, not a proof, and the thesis does not depend on it. The OpenFASoC tapeouts are the nearest prior art for silicon-versus-simulation on a parametric op-amp family; their measurement setup is reused rather than reinvented. If a slot is available at month 20 for something simple, take it early rather than late. Tiny Tapeout's SkyWater shuttles run again through ChipFoundry, now allow four-tile-high designs and run two shuttles at once, but schedules are outside my control.

**On the measurement problem, if the chip goes ahead.** The shared shuttle places an analog switch between the chip's pads and the design; the path is specified at under 500 ohms, under 5 picofarads and a maximum of 4 milliamps. For a high-impedance amplifier output that is not a small perturbation — it means measuring the design plus the switch. This needs either an on-chip buffer or a measurement arranged so the path cancels out, and either costs area on a 1×2 slot of about 160 by 225 micrometres, with metal 5 reserved for the shuttle's power grid. Design for this from the start or the measurement is worthless.

## 11. What if it does not work

A negative result is acceptable — but only a well-constructed one. "We tried and it did not work" is not a PhD, because an examiner will assume I did it badly.

Four things make a failure into a finding, and all four have to be designed in from the start:

1. Proof the information was there to be learned (§8).
2. Alternative approaches run alongside, so it is not just my architecture failing (Question 4).
3. An explanation of *why* it fails — for claim 2(i) the shortcut, for 2(ii) the target-pressure mechanism.
4. A further prediction from that explanation, then tested: for 2(i), that adding coverage along the constructed shortcut direction closes the gap without adding quantity; for 2(ii), that C-het and C1 restore the property and that C2 either does or does not, with the mechanism predicting which.

If C2 fails to restore the spread-defined properties while its latent still tracks the true per-chip draw, the finding is that a latent-variable objective can identify the hidden variable without installing the spread in the trunk — a sharper negative answer to the source paper's open question than the paper itself could have obtained, because the draw is certifiable here. If neither happens, the finding is that at least one latent-variable objective does not answer the open question in a domain with exact ground truth — still publishable, provided the C-het and C1 controls passed on the same data.

I also reframe the question deliberately. "Does this AI technique work on circuits?" is not useful, because nobody is currently using it, so a negative answer saves no one anything. "Which circuit properties are learnable, under what conditions, and which failures matter for search?" applies to every AI circuit model already in use, so the answer is useful whichever way it comes out.

**Three things to settle before submitting.**

First, the department and the supervisor's field. Computer science committees accept a characterisation thesis; electronic engineering committees often expect a working tool; a committee in Applied Physics, whose own work is at the device level, will read a study of AI-sized op-amps on an open silicon process as computer science unless the thesis contains something they recognise as theirs. Two pieces of this plan exist for that reason and would be weaker without it: the compact-model bridge of §10.3 and the computing-in-memory tile of Question 6. The repair loop is the working tool for the engineering audience. A co-supervisor in electronic design automation or machine learning is to be named before the plan is submitted. Four questions go to the supervisor first: which computing in memory they mean for this PhD — device-level and neuromorphic, which is what the group publishes, or macro- and architecture-level, which is what the survey literature means — and whether arrays or tapeouts are planned and in which process; whether the group holds measured device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle variability data on any device, and whether a student may model and publish it; whether a co-supervisor is acceptable and who; and whether the department will examine a thesis whose first paper is a measurement study on an open process. The answers to the first two decide whether §10.3 is real or cut.

Second, whether the survivability curve has already appeared in the post-layout-aware sizing literature, where it would most plausibly hide (§15).

Third, whether the AutoSizer authors have published the population number from their own harness since this was written.

## 12. Main risks

**The infrastructure eats a year.** Automating the layout and verification tools is where projects in this area historically lose time. Mitigation: one process only; fixed circuit families for the first paper; the pipeline build and the first publishable result are the same activity; and the ALIGN-plus-Magic path on this process already exists in AutoSizer's public code, so month two is a reproduction, not a build. If the pipeline cannot be made to work on the OSIRIS baseline placer as well, the first paper ships with ALIGN as the single policy and says so. If neither works, that is known by month three.

**The public dataset does less than hoped.** The 87,100-layout OSIRIS release covers five circuits at fixed sizing; what varies is the physical placement and the transistor finger arrangement, with frequency-response simulation only and no manufacturing variation or temperature. So the *dataset* is only a starting point for "predict what layout does to a fixed design." The *pipeline* is the asset: it is one of the two layout tools for the survivability study. The design-variation and variation-simulation infrastructure is budgeted as work to be done.

**The survival number depends on the layout policy.** Addressed by running two fixed policies (§9). The residual risk is a reviewer who says both single-pass layouts are bad and the number is therefore pessimistic. Answer: the best-of-N condition is reported alongside, and the gap between single-pass and best-of-N is itself informative about how much of "survival" is layout skill.

**The source paper is a five-week-old preprint.** If it does not replicate or is overturned, §4's framing weakens. Mitigation: claim 2(i) rests on shortcut learning, which does not depend on it; claim 2(ii)'s mechanism — a squared-error regressor answers with the conditional mean, so a spread is learned only when the objective rewards it — is elementary and does not depend on it either. What would be lost is the connection to the world-model literature, not the circuit results. The version number of the preprint is to be checked before citation (v1 of 29 July 2026 was read in full).

**The read-versus-use gap is trivial on fixed families.** On a fixed topology with a handful of sizing variables a small network fits nominal behaviour almost exactly, and the gap only appears on the constructed shortcut sets. Mitigation: the primary skeleton is one cross-topology model with held-out families (§3), where "does it use gain" is not trivial; the per-family models are a control that bounds the capacity explanation. If even the cross-topology model shows no gap outside the constructed sets, the finding is that these models are more faithful than the world-model literature would predict, reported as such.

**The read-versus-use gap is "just" out-of-distribution error.** It is, and the plan says so. The contribution is the per-property decomposition, the mechanism behind it, and whether it predicts search utility. If the decomposition adds nothing over aggregate out-of-distribution error, that is itself the finding for claim 2(iii), and claims 1 and 2(ii) stand independently.

**The conventional approach wins.** Expected and reported. The thesis is the map, not the method.

**Someone publishes first.** The nearest holder of the survivability number is the AutoSizer group at Brookhaven, whose public code already computes per-metric post-layout degradation on this process; GLOVA's group holds the schematic-level equivalent at 28 nm. Mitigation: start in month one, fixed families only, preprint by month four, submit by month six. If scooped, the curve-against-margin, the two-policy methodology, the failure decomposition and the out-of-sample pre-layout predictor are the differentiators, and the learnability work is unaffected.

**The differentiator gets absorbed.** If a systems group adds probing or representational analysis to an existing learned circuit model — FALCON's group, INSIGHT's group at UT Austin, or the Learn-by-Compare authors are the likeliest — the §4 contribution narrows sharply. Nothing prevents this. The partial defence is that the learnability control, the constructed-shortcut sets, the held-out-direction test and the certified per-chip draw are methodologically awkward and unrewarding to bolt onto a systems paper, so they are more likely to stay unoccupied than the headline number is. Watch for it, and if it happens, pivot to the diagnostic claim in Question 6, which is harder to retrofit.

**The group has no variability data, or will not share it.** Then the compact-model bridge is cut at the start of Year 2 and the Year-3 tile runs on the SkyWater resistive-memory primitive or a published statistical model. The thesis loses its most direct link to the supervisor's devices but none of its claims; the tile still tests claim 2(iii) in a variation-limited setting. This is the risk the §11 questions are designed to surface before the plan is submitted, not after.

**The computing-in-memory tile eats Year 3.** The tile has no automatic layout tool, and the read-path converter is a hard analog block. Mitigation: the tile is a *fixed* family with a scripted regular layout, one converter design taken from the open literature and held constant, and the design variables are array-level (size, resolution, conductance levels, wire width). If the extracted-layout path for the tile is not working by the middle of Year 3, the expensive check becomes the statistical simulation with analytical wire resistance, the tile result is reported at that fidelity, and the op-amp repair loop remains the primary demonstration of claim 2(iii).

**The sensitivity command does not work through the device wrappers.** ngspice's sensitivity analysis perturbs each device parameter numerically and warns of second-order effects on highly sensitive parameters; whether it reaches the width and length of a transistor wrapped in the open process's subcircuits is untested. Fallback: finite differences by re-simulation, seconds per design at the drawing-board stage. Arm B-S and the held-out-direction test survive either way.

**Expensive checks limit training data.** For claims 1 and 2(i): lots of cheap drawing-board simulations, a few expensive physical ones, chosen where the model is least certain. For claim 2(ii) the spread is learned from a few chips per design pooled across many designs (C-het), so the hundreds-of-chips labels are needed only for the C1 control and for an evaluation set (§13).

**The shuttle slot slips.** Outside my control and non-fatal, because the chip is not load-bearing.

One graphics card — the source study ran entirely on a single consumer card. Ordinary workstation compute for simulation; the population study is days, not months. Free public manufacturing kits. For the Year-2 bridge, access to the group's device measurements and probe station time for any additional variability sweeps.

**Monte Carlo budget, split by purpose, estimates to be replaced by measurement in Year 2.** Two different things need Monte Carlo and they cost differently.

- *Learnability, claim 2(ii)*: the mechanism does not need layout, so these chips are simulated at the drawing-board stage at one to three seconds each. C-het: about twenty thousand designs times eight chips, around 160,000 simulations, roughly 50–130 core-hours. C1 labels and the shared evaluation set: about five hundred designs times two hundred chips (the relative error on a spread is roughly one over the square root of twice the sample count, about five percent at two hundred), around 100,000 simulations, 30–80 core-hours. Both are hours on a thirty-two-core workstation.
- *Survival, claim 1*: this one does need post-layout chips at ten to sixty seconds each. A two-stage scheme keeps it bounded: fifty chips for every design in the population, then two hundred for the designs whose pass fraction lands within the uncertainty band of the specification threshold. For a two-thousand-design population with a quarter in the band, that is about 175,000 post-layout simulations, roughly 500–2,900 core-hours — under a day to four days on thirty-two cores. It scales linearly with the population and is scheduled, not assumed.
- *The computing-in-memory tile, Year 3*: the expensive check is one statistical array simulation per candidate tile, with the mapped network's accuracy evaluated over sampled cell draws. Its cost depends on array size and is measured in the first month of the tile work before the loop's budget is fixed; the statistical simulators the field uses run such evaluations in minutes, and a SPICE-level extracted array is the slow ceiling.

If a chip goes ahead: a 1×2-tile design with four analog pins on the shared shuttle costs 420 euros at current published prices (two tiles at 70 each, the first two pins at 40 each, the next two at 100 each), plus the development kit, board and shipping. Call it under a thousand all in.

## 14. Deliberately left out

Recorded so they read as decisions rather than oversights.

**AI-invented topologies as a second survivability paper.** Each new topology needs its own layout template, and the layout tools fail on unfamiliar circuits, so a topology population would measure the layout tool as much as the designs. The topology datasets (AnalogGenie, OCB, AMSNet, Masala-CHAI) are listed in §10.1 as sources should a later project want them. The Year-3 slot this would have taken goes to the computing-in-memory tile, which tests the plan's claims where they are load-bearing and connects the thesis to the supervisor's field; a second op-amp population does neither.

**Testing whether a model transfers between manufacturing processes.** glayout generates the same op-amp for SkyWater 130 nm and GlobalFoundries 180 nm, and the 180 nm statistical models are the best documented of the open kits, so a one-family transfer curve would be cheap. It is left out because it answers a question nobody has asked of this thesis, and Year 3 has one optional slot, which the tile takes.

**A full pivot to device-to-array co-design of computing-in-memory hardware.** The supervisor's field, and a legitimate PhD — but a different one, which many groups with fabs are already doing along the lines the survey literature lays out (manufacturing maturity, error correction, compilers, hardware–software co-design). The question this plan asks — what a learned model uses versus contains, and whether that predicts search cost — is the part neither the analog-design community nor the computing-in-memory community is doing, and it is the part that survives a scoop. Computing in memory enters as the application family and the bridge, not as the thesis.

**A learned device model as ground truth.** Neural-network compact models make device-level Monte Carlo cheap (§3), and it would be tempting to use one to shrink the §13 budget. Using a surrogate as the ground truth for claim 1 would make the survival number a statement about the surrogate. They enter only as a cheap fidelity in Question 6 and as the bridge deliverable of §10.3.

**Breaking circuits into blocks and handling each separately.** Sensible-sounding, and decades old. The usual argument for why it plateaus is physical: the approach works when the pieces barely affect each other, and analog circuits are built so the pieces strongly affect each other. That argument is a prior, not evidence, and recent systems that reason about sub-structures do report gains, so it is held loosely. Revisit if the flat approach hits a size limit. (The same coupling argument is why the intervention test needs the large-change construction in §7: properties that strongly affect each other cannot be moved independently except in the small.)

**Merging a language model into the core model.** Done already in robotics; porting it is application, not discovery. The language model stays outside as the proposer in the repair loop of Question 6, which is where the original project scope wanted it; the change model is what is studied.

**Silicon as evidence for variation claims.** One chip is one sample. It stays a demonstration.

**Distributional objectives that predict a mean and a variance, as a test of the open question.** In circuits such an objective's optimum *is* the spread, so it is the known cure, not a test of the open question. Included as C-het, a control; not counted as a test of the open question.

**A latent-predictive (JEPA-style) model as the core object.** Its self-supervised motivation is weak for a dozen exact scalars that cost a second each. Included only as arm D, to test one transferred prediction of the source paper; cut first if time is short.

**Aging and time-dependent drift.** None of the three open processes ships a validated aging model. Stays outside all three papers; stated as a boundary. For the computing-in-memory tile this excludes conductance drift and endurance, which are central to that field; the tile study is a study of programming-time and read-time variation only, and says so.

**The other research threads in my reading.** The fab-process and epitaxy papers (RHEED analysis, autonomous growth, self-driving deposition) belong to a separate project on learned process surrogates and do not bear on this plan. One possible link is recorded and not pursued: if the group grows the films for the device in §10.3, the same variability data feeds both a growth-to-device model and the compact model.
